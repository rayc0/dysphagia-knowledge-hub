# Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD) and Dysphagia — Clinical Guide to Swallowing Problems in FTD

> **TL;DR:** Swallowing problems in frontotemporal dementia are common, under-recognised, and often silent. A landmark FEES study found moderate swallowing abnormalities in 12 of 21 FTD patients even though caregivers reported difficulty in only 4 — meaning most FTD dysphagia is invisible until it causes aspiration. The bigger daily danger, however, is behavioural: hyperorality, binge eating, food gorging, and eating non-food objects drive choking and aspiration risk long before the swallow reflex itself fails. Management combines texture modification (IDDSI framework), strict mealtime supervision, eating-behaviour control, and early advance care planning. Feeding tubes do not extend life or prevent pneumonia in advanced dementia.

## What is frontotemporal dementia?

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a group of neurodegenerative diseases caused by progressive damage to the frontal and temporal lobes of the brain — the regions that govern personality, behaviour, language, and executive function. It is the second most common cause of young-onset dementia (under 65), typically striking between ages 45 and 65, earlier than Alzheimer's disease.

FTD is not one disease but a spectrum. The three main clinical syndromes are:

- **Behavioural variant FTD (bvFTD)** — the most common form (~50% of cases), dominated by personality change, apathy, disinhibition, loss of empathy, and marked changes in eating behaviour. Also historically called Pick's disease.
- **Primary progressive aphasia (PPA)** — language-led variants, including the non-fluent/agrammatic variant (nfvPPA) and the semantic variant (svPPA, also called semantic dementia).
- **FTD with motor overlap** — FTD frequently co-occurs with motor disorders: progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal syndrome (CBS), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS-FTD spectrum). These motor-overlap forms carry the highest and earliest dysphagia risk.

The current diagnostic benchmark is the **2011 revised bvFTD criteria of Rascovsky and colleagues** (International Behavioural Variant FTD Criteria Consortium), published in *Brain*. These criteria define "possible" bvFTD by three of six clinical features — disinhibition, apathy, loss of sympathy/empathy, perseverative compulsive behaviour, hyperorality and altered food preference, and executive dysfunction — and "probable" bvFTD by adding functional decline plus frontotemporal atrophy or hypometabolism on imaging. Notably, **hyperorality and changed food preference is one of the six core diagnostic features** — eating disturbance is not a side issue in FTD; it is built into the definition of the disease.

Median survival from symptom onset is roughly **6 to 12 years** depending on phenotype. Onyike's 2011 Johns Hopkins review reported median survival of about 11.8 years overall (10.5 years in bvFTD, 12.6 years in non-fluent PPA), while Kansal's 2016 meta-analysis of FTD phenotypes found shorter survival in syndromes with motor-neuron involvement (ALS-FTD) and longer survival in semantic dementia. Dysphagia, when it appears, is a marker of progression toward brainstem involvement and shorter remaining survival.

## How common is dysphagia in FTD — and why is it missed?

The single most important primary study is **Langa and colleagues (2006, *Archives of Neurology*)**, the first systematic instrumental assessment of swallowing in frontotemporal lobar degeneration. Twenty-one consecutive FTD-spectrum patients (9 bvFTD, 7 progressive non-fluent aphasia, 5 semantic dementia) underwent fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) with both liquids and food.

The findings changed how clinicians think about FTD and swallowing:

- Caregivers reported swallowing difficulty in only **4 of 21** patients (~19%).
- Instrumental examination revealed **moderate swallowing abnormalities in 12 of 21** patients (~57%).
- The abnormalities were **not explained by compulsive eating behaviour** — they reflected genuine deficits in cortical and subcortical pathways connecting to the brainstem swallowing centre.

The authors' conclusion is the key clinical message: **when measured by instrumentation, swallowing abnormalities are present in many FTD patients, and the appearance of dysphagia signals progression of the disease toward brainstem systems.** In plain terms — most FTD dysphagia is silent. Families do not see it, patients do not report it (insight is characteristically lost in bvFTD), and it surfaces only when aspiration pneumonia or a choking event forces the issue.

This mirrors the wider Lewy-body and parkinsonism literature — the same pattern of high instrumental prevalence against low subjective report is seen in dementia with Lewy bodies (Londos 2013: 32% subjective vs 92% instrument-confirmed) and across the atypical parkinsonisms. FTD belongs squarely in this "silent dysphagia" cluster.

## The two distinct swallowing problems in FTD

FTD produces dysphagia through **two separate mechanisms**, and good care requires recognising both.

### 1. Behavioural eating disturbance (early and mid-stage)

Changes in eating behaviour are a diagnostic hallmark of bvFTD and are extensively documented by Ahmed and colleagues (2016, *JAMA Neurology*) and the Association for Frontotemporal Degeneration (AFTD) clinical literature. These include:

- **Hyperorality** — putting food (and non-food objects) into the mouth excessively or compulsively.
- **Binge eating and food gorging** — eating rapidly, stuffing the mouth, taking the next mouthful before swallowing the last.
- **Carbohydrate and sweet craving** — a marked shift toward sweet, energy-dense foods.
- **Rigid food fads** — eating only one or two specific foods, sometimes in ritualised patterns.
- **Pica-like behaviour** — attempting to eat inedible objects.
- **Excessive fluid or alcohol intake.**

Each of these behaviours raises choking and aspiration risk independently of the swallow reflex itself. A patient who stuffs their mouth with food faster than they can clear it will aspirate even with a neurologically intact pharyngeal swallow. This is why FTD choking events often happen **early** in the disease, years before the reflexive swallow deteriorates.

### 2. Neurogenic dysphagia (mid to late stage)

As FTD pathology spreads from frontal/temporal cortex toward subcortical and brainstem structures, the reflexive swallow itself deteriorates. The Langa 2006 FEES series documented pharyngeal-phase abnormalities — delayed initiation, residue, and penetration — consistent with cortical-subcortical disconnection of the brainstem swallowing centre. In FTD with motor overlap (PSP, CBS, ALS-FTD), neurogenic dysphagia arrives **earlier and more severely**: PSP and CBS add parkinsonian swallowing dysfunction, while ALS-FTD adds bulbar motor-neuron failure (tongue atrophy, weak palate, poor laryngeal closure).

The practical staging implication: **behavioural eating risk dominates early; neurogenic dysphagia risk dominates late; both must be managed throughout.**

## Warning signs caregivers should watch for

Because FTD dysphagia is often silent, caregivers should not wait for coughing. Watch for:

- **Pocketing** — food held in the cheeks or under the tongue after the meal ends.
- **Prolonged meals** — taking much longer to finish than before.
- **Wet or gurgly voice** after eating or drinking.
- **Unexplained weight loss** despite apparently adequate intake.
- **Recurrent chest infections or low-grade fevers** — the classic presentation of chronic silent aspiration.
- **Food avoidance** — quietly rejecting textures that are hard to clear (stringy meat, dry crumbly food, mixed textures).
- **Choking or coughing fits during binge/gorging episodes** — a red flag that behavioural eating is outpacing swallow capacity.
- **Drooling or food spillage** from the mouth.

Any of these warrants referral for an instrumental swallowing assessment — **FEES or videofluoroscopy (VFSS)** — not just bedside observation. Given the Langa finding that caregiver report misses most cases, a low threshold for instrumental assessment is justified in FTD.

## How dysphagia is assessed in FTD

- **FEES (fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing)** — the method used in the Langa study; well tolerated, no radiation, and directly visualises residue, penetration, and aspiration. Particularly useful in FTD because it can be repeated to track progression.
- **VFSS / videofluoroscopy (modified barium swallow)** — the complementary standard; shows the full oral-pharyngeal sequence and quantifies timing.
- **Standardised screening tools** — EAT-10, GUSS, and water-swallow tests can flag risk but will under-detect in FTD because they rely partly on patient report and cooperation, both impaired in bvFTD.
- **Behavioural observation at meals** — in FTD this is as important as the instrumental study: the speech-language pathologist should observe a real meal to see gorging speed, mouth-stuffing, and supervision needs.

## Managing eating and drinking day to day

### Control the eating behaviour first

Because behavioural disturbance drives early risk, the first-line interventions are environmental:

- **Serve one small portion at a time** — do not put a full plate in front of a patient who gorges. Refill after each portion is swallowed.
- **Pace the meal** — remove the next item until the mouth is clear; use verbal and visual cues.
- **Limit access between meals** — lock or relocate food storage if binge eating or pica is severe.
- **Supervise all meals** — an FTD patient with hyperorality should not eat unsupervised.
- **Manage sweet craving safely** — offer IDDSI-appropriate sweet options (smooth puddings, puréed fruit) rather than hard, crumbly, or chewy sweets.

### Modify texture using the IDDSI framework

As neurogenic dysphagia emerges, progress food texture and drink thickness along the **International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI)** framework under speech-language-pathologist guidance:

- **IDDSI Level 6 (soft & bite-sized)** and **Level 5 (minced & moist)** for mild-moderate impairment.
- **IDDSI Level 4 (puréed)** and **Level 3 (liquidised)** as pharyngeal dysfunction advances.
- **Drinks** thickened to the prescribed IDDSI level (0–4) if thin-liquid aspiration is confirmed instrumentally — but do not thicken by default; thickened fluids carry hydration and quality-of-life costs.

### Support the swallow directly

- **Upright positioning** at 90 degrees for all intake, maintained for 30 minutes after meals.
- **Compensatory strategies** prescribed by the speech-language pathologist (chin tuck, effortful swallow, alternating solids and sips).
- **Meticulous oral care** — toothbrushing and oral hygiene reduce aspiration-pneumonia risk; the classic Yoneyama 2002 randomised trial in institutionalised older adults showed daily professional oral care significantly lowered pneumonia incidence.
- **Treat sialorrhea (drooling)** if present, and review medications that dry the mouth or sedate.

## The feeding-tube question in FTD

Families of people with advanced FTD will almost always face the gastrostomy (PEG) decision. The evidence base is consistent and should be shared honestly:

- In **advanced dementia**, feeding tubes have **not been shown to extend survival, prevent aspiration pneumonia, heal pressure injuries, or improve comfort or quality of life**. Goldberg's 2014 critical review of gastrostomy in advanced dementia with dysphagia reported median post-PEG survival measured in months, not years, in the dementia subgroup.
- Tube feeding **does not eliminate aspiration risk** — patients can still aspirate saliva and refluxed gastric contents; published aspiration-pneumonia rates in tube-fed patients range widely (4–95% across studies) with substantial mortality.
- FTD-specific nuance: because FTD often strikes younger patients and can coexist with ALS (where tube feeding has a clearer role), the decision must be individualised. In **ALS-FTD with bulbar failure but preserved cognition and a short expected decline**, PEG may be appropriate. In **advanced bvFTD with severe behavioural disturbance**, tubes are frequently pulled out, cause distress, and do not improve outcomes.

The mainstream palliative-dementia recommendation is **careful hand feeding / comfort feeding** for as long as it provides pleasure, combined with **early advance care planning (ACP)** — documenting the patient's wishes about artificial nutrition **before** capacity is lost. Because FTD impairs insight and judgement early, ACP conversations should happen at diagnosis, not at crisis.

## Common mistakes and pitfalls

1. **Waiting for coughing.** Most FTD dysphagia is silent. Absence of coughing does not mean absence of aspiration — request instrumental assessment (FEES/VFSS) on clinical suspicion alone.
2. **Treating the swallow but ignoring the behaviour.** Texture modification alone will not protect a patient who gorges. Portion control, pacing, and supervision are the primary early interventions.
3. **Assuming reported intake equals safe intake.** Caregiver report under-detects by a wide margin in FTD; trust the instrumental study, not the dinner-table impression.
4. **Thickening all fluids by default.** Confirm thin-liquid aspiration instrumentally first; unnecessary thickening worsens hydration and quality of life.
5. **Delaying advance care planning.** bvFTD erodes insight and decision-making capacity early. Document wishes about artificial nutrition at diagnosis.
6. **Reaching for a feeding tube in advanced disease.** The evidence does not support survival or pneumonia-prevention benefit in advanced dementia; comfort feeding plus ACP is the standard of care.
7. **Missing the motor-overlap red flags.** New tongue atrophy, fasciculations, falls, or gaze palsy in an FTD patient suggest ALS-FTD, PSP, or CBS overlap — these change both the dysphagia prognosis and the management plan.
