# Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) and Dysphagia — Clinical Guide to Swallowing Problems in Lewy Body Dementia

> **TL;DR:** Swallowing problems affect roughly one-third to one-half of people with dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and objective testing finds dysfunction in most patients who report symptoms. DLB dysphagia is dominated by **pharyngeal-phase** problems — delayed swallow initiation, residue, and penetration/aspiration — and up to one in three patients with measurable impairment feels nothing wrong (silent dysphagia). Pneumonia is the single most common cause of death in DLB, so early instrumental screening (VFSS or FEES), texture modification, and oral hygiene matter more here than in almost any other dementia.

## What is dementia with Lewy bodies?

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is the second most common degenerative dementia after Alzheimer's disease. It is caused by abnormal clumps of the protein alpha-synuclein — called Lewy bodies — building up in brain cells that control thinking, movement, alertness, and autonomic functions such as blood pressure, digestion, and swallowing.

The 2017 international consensus criteria (McKeith and colleagues, the DLB Consortium) define DLB by a core dementia syndrome plus four cardinal features:

1. **Fluctuating cognition** — unpredictable swings in attention and alertness, sometimes hour to hour
2. **Recurrent visual hallucinations** — well-formed, detailed, often of people or animals
3. **REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD)** — physically acting out dreams, often years before dementia begins
4. **Parkinsonism** — slowness, stiffness, and sometimes tremor

DLB sits on a spectrum with Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD). The practical rule: if dementia appears **before or within one year** of parkinsonism, the diagnosis is DLB; if parkinsonism has been established for more than a year before dementia develops, it is PDD. Average survival after DLB diagnosis is about 4–8 years, somewhat shorter than Alzheimer's disease.

## How common are swallowing problems in DLB?

The numbers depend on how you look, and the gap between the two methods is itself a clinical lesson:

- **Ask the patient:** In the largest videofluoroscopic study of DLB/PDD (82 consecutive patients, Lund University, Sweden, published in *BMC Neurology* 2013), 26 of 82 patients (32%) reported subjective swallowing symptoms.
- **Test the patient:** Of those 26 symptomatic patients, 24 (92%) had confirmed swallowing dysfunction on videofluoroscopic examination (VFSE). A follow-up carbonated-liquid study from the same group found objective dysfunction in 40 of 48 patients (83%) — and crucially, **35% of patients with measurable impairment had no subjective complaint at all**.
- **Population studies:** A 2017 Hong Kong study of Chinese DLB patients found dysphagia in 52%, with aspiration pneumonia in 26%. Every patient who developed aspiration pneumonia had dysphagia, versus only 35% of those who did not.

The takeaway: relying on "does food go down OK?" misses a third of cases. In DLB, the absence of coughing does **not** mean the absence of aspiration.

## What does DLB dysphagia look like on testing?

The Lund VFSE series gives the clearest picture of the DLB swallowing signature. Among patients with confirmed dysfunction:

- **Pharyngeal-phase dysfunction in 88–96%** — the dominant pattern:
  - Delayed initiation of the pharyngeal swallow (62%)
  - Retention of material in the pharynx after the swallow (50%)
  - Penetration or aspiration of material toward or into the airway (42%)
- **Oral-phase dysfunction in about 50%** — slow or fragmented bolus transport, drooling, difficulty forming a cohesive bolus

This profile differs from Alzheimer's disease, where oral-phase problems (holding food in the mouth, forgetting to swallow, pocketing) tend to dominate, and from Parkinson's disease, where rigidity and incomplete hyolaryngeal excursion are typical. DLB combines the parkinsonian pharyngeal deficit with the cognitive and attentional problems of a fluctuating dementia — a patient may swallow safely when alert and aspirate during a fluctuation "off" period.

Autonomic failure, a core DLB feature, adds another layer: reduced saliva control, slowed pharyngeal reflexes, and discoordination of the breathing–swallowing rhythm. Case reports of autopsy-proven Lewy body disease presenting with **severe dysphagia as the first symptom** exist, so new swallowing difficulty in an undiagnosed older adult with hallucinations or dream enactment should prompt neurological referral, not just a soft diet.

## Why it matters: pneumonia is the leading cause of death

The stakes in DLB dysphagia are unusually high:

- An autopsy series of DLB patients found **pneumonia was the cause of death in 53.8%** — far ahead of renal failure (15.4%) and respiratory failure (12.8%). Median survival from onset was 8 years.
- In the Lund follow-up, 12 of the 24 patients with confirmed swallowing dysfunction died during the study period; **9 of the 12 died of pneumonia**. Patients with dysphagia had significantly higher pneumonia mortality (p = 0.034).
- Aspiration pneumonia in advanced dementia carries a mortality rate reported as high as 62%.

Aspiration in DLB is often **silent** — no cough, no throat clearing, no distress — because the same sensory blunting that affects other reflexes dampens the laryngeal cough trigger. Caregivers should treat recurrent "chest infections," unexplained fevers, or a wet, gurgly voice after meals as possible aspiration events, not bad luck.

## How is DLB dysphagia assessed?

**Screening.** The EAT-10 questionnaire (10 items, scored 0–40; a score of 3 or more is abnormal) is a practical first step and is recommended in DLB caregiver guidance. Because of silent dysphagia, many clinicians screen all DLB patients at diagnosis rather than waiting for complaints.

**Instrumental assessment.** The two gold-standard tests:

- **VFSS / VFSE (videofluoroscopic swallowing study):** moving X-ray of swallows of different textures; shows delay, residue, penetration, and aspiration directly. This was the method used in the Lund DLB studies.
- **FEES (fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing):** a scope through the nose watches the pharynx before and after the swallow; no radiation, repeatable at the bedside, and better for seeing secretion pooling and vocal-fold movement.

Given the fluctuating nature of DLB, a single "passed" bedside water test on a good day can be falsely reassuring. If suspicion is high — recurrent pneumonia, weight loss, wet voice — push for instrumental assessment even when screening looks normal.

## Management: what actually helps

There is no drug that treats DLB dysphagia itself. Management is compensatory, rehabilitative, and protective:

**1. Texture modification (IDDSI framework).** Match food and fluid texture to the instrumental findings. Pharyngeal-delay patterns with thin-liquid penetration usually start with mildly to moderately thickened fluids (IDDSI Level 1–3) and minced-moist or puréed solids (IDDSI Level 4–5), stepped up or down as the disease progresses. Re-test after any step change — thickening by default without assessment can worsen residue and dehydration.

**2. Compensatory techniques.** Chin-down (chin-tuck) posture narrows the airway entrance during the swallow and is a standard first-line manoeuvre for delayed pharyngeal swallow. Small bites, slow pacing, alternating sips and bites, and a fully upright 90-degree seated position during — and for at least 30 minutes after — meals are basic protective rules.

**3. Carbonated liquids — a DLB-specific finding.** The Lund group's 2017 study found that carbonated liquid **improved swallowing in 87%** of DLB/PDD patients with pharyngeal dysfunction, likely because carbonation provides stronger sensory stimulation to a blunted pharynx. This is a clinician-supervised strategy, not a home remedy — but it is one of the few DLB-specific swallowing interventions with direct evidence.

**4. Swallowing therapy and strength training.** Expiratory muscle strength training (EMST) and video-assisted swallowing therapy have evidence in the Lewy body spectrum for improving cough effectiveness and swallow safety. A speech-language pathologist (speech and language therapist) should individualise the programme; effortful-swallow and Mendelsohn exercises require enough cognition to follow commands, which fluctuates in DLB.

**5. Oral hygiene.** Aspiration pneumonia is caused by bacteria from the mouth riding aspirated material into the lungs. The landmark Yoneyama 2002 randomised trial in nursing-home residents showed that professional oral care cut pneumonia dramatically. Twice-daily toothbrushing and regular dental review are among the highest-value, lowest-cost interventions available.

**6. Medication review.** DLB patients are exquisitely sensitive to antidopaminergic drugs — antipsychotics such as haloperidol can cause severe, sometimes fatal, worsening of parkinsonism and swallowing. Anticholinergics dry the mouth and worsen bolus formation. Every new swallowing complaint deserves a medication review before it is attributed to disease progression.

**7. Manage the fluctuations.** Schedule main meals during the patient's most alert periods. If a meal coincides with a fluctuation — staring, unresponsive, markedly drowsy — pause feeding and resume when alertness returns. Feeding through a low-alertness episode is a direct aspiration risk.

## The feeding-tube question in DLB

Families will eventually face the question of tube feeding. The evidence base is consistent and worth knowing early: in advanced dementia, **no study has shown that feeding tubes extend survival, prevent pneumonia, improve nutrition, or improve quality of life**. Aspiration of saliva and refluxed tube feed continues regardless. Major geriatric and palliative guidelines therefore recommend careful hand feeding (comfort feeding) for as long as it is tolerated, with goals-of-care conversations held early — while the person with DLB can still participate.

Because DLB survival after dysphagia onset can still be measured in years rather than months, the decision is more individualised than in end-stage Alzheimer's: a percutaneous gastrostomy (PEG) may be reasonable for selected patients with severe pharyngeal failure but preserved cognition and quality of life. This is a conversation for the neurology, speech pathology, and palliative care teams together — and for an advance care plan made before a crisis forces it.

## Common mistakes and pitfalls

1. **Waiting for coughing.** A third of objectively impaired DLB patients feel nothing. Screen at diagnosis, not at first chest infection.
2. **One-off bedside clearance.** Fluctuations mean a good-day water test can miss bad-day aspiration. Recurrent pneumonia overrides a normal screen.
3. **Thickening everything by default.** Unassessed thickening increases pharyngeal residue and dehydration risk. Match texture to VFSS/FEES findings and re-test.
4. **Feeding through drowsy periods.** Pause during fluctuations; resume when alert. Never feed a semi-responsive patient.
5. **Antipsychotic prescriptions for hallucinations.** Typical antipsychotics can catastrophically worsen DLB parkinsonism and swallowing — flag the diagnosis to every prescriber.
6. **Treating "chest infections" as isolated events.** Recurrent pneumonia in DLB is a swallowing problem until proven otherwise; ask for instrumental assessment.
7. **Late goals-of-care talks.** Pneumonia causes over half of DLB deaths. Discuss feeding wishes and advance care plans early, while the patient can contribute.
