# Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) and Dysphagia — Clinical Guide to Swallowing Problems After Head Injury

> **TL;DR:** Between a quarter and two-thirds of people hospitalised with traumatic brain injury develop dysphagia — rising to roughly 68–76% in severe TBI. Unlike stroke, TBI dysphagia is complicated by cognitive and behavioural problems (impulsivity, agitation, poor insight) that make mealtimes dangerous even when the swallow mechanics recover. Most survivors regain functional swallowing within 3–6 months, but recovery tracks the **Rancho Los Amigos cognitive level**, not just the brain scan. Instrumental assessment (VFSS or FEES) is essential because silent aspiration is common.

## Why TBI causes dysphagia

Traumatic brain injury damages the swallowing system at multiple levels at once. A stroke usually hits one vascular territory; TBI is typically **diffuse** — cortical contusions, diffuse axonal injury, brainstem involvement, and often cranial nerve damage all in the same patient. The result is a swallowing disorder that mixes:

- **Neurogenic dysphagia** — impaired oral control, delayed pharyngeal trigger, reduced laryngeal elevation, weak airway protection
- **Cognitive-communication deficits** — poor attention, impulsivity, reduced insight, memory problems that sabotage safe-eating strategies
- **Behavioural disturbance** — agitation, disinhibition, cramming food, refusing to sit, pulling at tubes
- **Iatrogenic factors** — prolonged intubation, tracheostomy, sedation, and ICU-acquired weakness all compound the problem

This combination is why TBI dysphagia is managed differently from stroke dysphagia even when the videofluoroscopy looks similar. A Korean comparison study of severe TBI versus stroke patients found the VFSS abnormalities were broadly comparable — aspiration/penetration in 73.2% of the TBI group, decreased laryngeal elevation in 68.3%, and reduced epiglottic inversion in 61% — but the TBI patients were younger, more often cognitively impaired, and presented different rehabilitation challenges (Kim et al., *Ann Rehabil Med*, PMC4951361).

## How common is dysphagia after TBI?

Reported figures vary widely because studies use different definitions, timings, and patient mixes:

| Population | Dysphagia rate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Adult TBI, inpatient rehabilitation (all severities) | **25–93%** across studies | Howle et al. 2014 review, *Curr Phys Med Rehabil Rep* |
| Adult TBI, South African cohort | **32%**, of whom 28% aspirated | UCT cohort study |
| Severe TBI (GCS ≤8), VFSS-confirmed | **~43%** (42.8% of 42 patients) | Kim et al., *Ann Rehabil Med* |
| Severe TBI, ISPRM poster series (n=16) | **68.75%** dysphagia; 62.5% aspiration on VFSS | ISPRM 2018 |
| Pediatric TBI, all admissions | 3.8–5.3% overall | Morgan et al. 2003 |
| Pediatric **severe** TBI | **68–76%** | Morgan et al. 2003; Mageandran et al. 2009 |
| Pediatric TBI after brain surgery (Taiwan, nationwide) | 12.3% severe dysphagia | Huang 2014, *J Head Trauma Rehabil* |
| Subdural hematoma specifically | ~13% | *Front Neurol* 2021 |

The wide adult range reflects case-mix: mild TBI rarely causes dysphagia, while severe TBI with brainstem involvement almost always does. The consistent message across studies is that **severity drives prevalence** — and that many cases are missed without instrumental assessment.

## The silent aspiration problem

As in other neurogenic dysphagias, coughing is an unreliable screen. Mackay et al. (1999) found that among TBI patients referred for videofluoroscopy, 71% of studies were abnormal — including silent aspiration that bedside evaluation had not detected. A one-year longitudinal follow-up by Terré & Mearin (2009) tracked tracheal aspiration in severe TBI and found that aspiration evolved over time; persistent aspiration at one year correlated with baseline cognitive severity on the Rancho Los Amigos Scale.

Reduced urge to cough and blunted cough responses have been documented even in people with **remote** TBI and tracheostomy (PMC5059551), which means the airway's last line of defence may be impaired long after the acute phase. Practical consequence: **do not clear a TBI patient for oral intake on bedside assessment alone** if the injury was moderate or severe — request VFSS or FEES.

## The Rancho Los Amigos Scale governs feeding decisions

The single most useful framework for TBI feeding decisions is the **Rancho Los Amigos Scale (RLAS)** of cognitive recovery:

- **RLAS I–III** (coma to localised response): no oral feeding. Nutrition via NG tube or PEG. Oral care and early swallowing therapy only.
- **RLAS IV (Confused–Agitated)**: the dangerous stage. Patients may be physically able to chew and swallow but are impulsive, distractible, may cram food, grab others' food, eat non-food items, or pull out feeding tubes. Most protocols require emergence from RLAS IV before initiating oral feeding trials.
- **RLAS V (Confused–Inappropriate, non-agitated)**: supervised feeding trials can usually begin. Simple one-step cues, quiet environment, small portions, constant supervision.
- **RLAS VI (Confused–Appropriate)**: patients can follow simple directions consistently — the level at which total oral feeding typically becomes realistic.
- **RLAS VII–VIII**: supervised then independent eating, with residual need for texture modification depending on instrumental findings.

Mackay et al. identified RLAS as the **most important independent predictor** of time to full oral feeding — more predictive than CT findings or ventilation days. Behavioural problems affect eating in roughly 31% of severe TBI patients, and about 37% of severe TBI patients have not recovered an unrestricted diet at 18 weeks.

## What VFSS typically shows in TBI

Instrumental studies of TBI dysphagia consistently report a **mixed oral-pharyngeal pattern**:

- **Oral phase**: prolonged transit, poor bolus control, premature spillage, residue — reflecting lingual incoordination and reduced oral sensation
- **Pharyngeal phase**: delayed swallow trigger, reduced laryngeal elevation, incomplete epiglottic inversion, vallecular and pyriform residue, penetration and aspiration (often silent)
- **Piecemeal swallowing** and multiple swallows per bolus are common

Because TBI patients are often young with strong compensatory capacity, texture modification plus compensatory strategies frequently allows earlier oral intake than the raw impairment might suggest — but only under instrumental guidance.

## Recovery timeline

The prognosis is substantially better than in progressive neurological disease:

- **Most TBI survivors regain functional swallowing within 3–6 months**, with the steepest gains in the first 6 months and slower improvement thereafter (Terré & Mearin; Groher, *Dysphagia: Clinical Management in Adults and Children*)
- Post-extubation dysphagia from intubation alone (without structural brain injury to swallowing networks) typically resolves in **2–5 days**
- In the Terré & Mearin longitudinal study, **no silent aspiration remained at 1-year follow-up** in their severe-TBI cohort
- Predictors of **delayed** recovery: GCS <8.5, low RLAS, delayed time to follow commands, poor sitting balance, brainstem injury or midline shift on CT, temporal lobe haematoma, prolonged ventilation (>1.5–2 weeks), tracheostomy, older age
- A 2024 study (n=160) found that at 12 weeks post-injury, **older age, delayed command-following, and poor sitting balance** predicted continued NG-tube dependence

Note the conflicting evidence: some studies find tracheostomy and ventilation duration are markers of injury severity rather than independent causes of persistent dysphagia. The practical takeaway is to reassess regularly rather than assume permanence.

## Management principles

**1. Instrumental assessment early and repeatedly.** VFSS or FEES once medically stable and sufficiently alert; repeat as cognition improves through RLAS levels. FEES is often more practical in the ICU and in agitated patients.

**2. Match the diet to the instrumental findings, not the diagnosis.** IDDSI levels are assigned from VFSS/FEES results — commonly Level 5 (minced & moist) or Level 4 (puréed) during early recovery, upgrading as swallowing and cognition improve. See the hub's [IDDSI framework guide](../iddsi/iddsi-framework-complete-guide.md) for level definitions and testing methods.

**3. Manage the environment, not just the bolus.** For RLAS IV–V patients: quiet room, minimal distractions, one caregiver, one-step verbal cues, small utensils, remove non-food items from reach, allow extra time. Supervision is non-negotiable until consistent RLAS VI.

**4. Watch for behavioural red flags.** Cramming, stealing food, eating too fast, refusing to sit, and pocketing food are cognitive problems, not swallowing problems — but they cause choking and aspiration. Strategies: controlled plate presentation, portion pacing, finger foods only if safe, and never leaving high-risk foods within reach.

**5. Tracheostomy considerations.** A tracheostomy tube tethers the larynx and blunts subglottic sensation; cuff deflation and speaking-valve trials (e.g., Passy-Muir) often improve swallowing safety and should be coordinated with the respiratory team. See the hub's [tracheostomy and dysphagia guide](tracheostomy-and-dysphagia-management.md).

**6. Nutrition and hydration.** Early enteral feeding protects gut function and supports neurorecovery. As oral intake resumes, monitor for the classic gap between "safe" and "adequate" — a patient may swallow safely but eat too slowly or too little to meet needs, requiring supplementary tube feeds during the transition.

**7. Oral hygiene.** Aspiration pneumonia risk tracks oral bacterial load; structured oral care reduces pneumonia incidence in dependent patients (Yoneyama 2002 RCT). See the hub's [oral care guide](../caregiving/oral-care-dysphagia-aspiration-pneumonia-prevention.md).

## Common mistakes / Pitfalls

1. **Clearing on bedside assessment alone.** Silent aspiration is frequent in TBI; moderate-to-severe injuries need VFSS/FEES before oral feeding.
2. **Feeding during RLAS IV agitation.** Physical ability to swallow does not equal safe feeding — impulsivity and cramming cause choking events. Wait for emergence from the agitated stage, or use only tightly controlled trials.
3. **Treating it like stroke.** TBI patients are often young, cognitively impaired, and behaviourally unpredictable; environmental and behavioural management matters as much as texture.
4. **Assuming the tracheostomy is the whole problem.** The tube contributes, but the underlying brain injury drives the dysphagia — decannulation alone does not normalise the swallow.
5. **Stopping reassessment too early.** Swallowing improves for 6–12 months; a diet level that was right at week 4 is often unnecessarily restrictive at week 16. Schedule repeat instrumental assessment.
6. **Ignoring the safe-but-inadequate gap.** A patient cleared for oral intake may still not eat enough; track weight and intake, and keep supplementary feeding until oral intake is demonstrably sufficient.
7. **Blaming non-compliance.** Refusing thickened fluids or modified food in TBI is usually impaired insight, not stubbornness — address it with supervision structure and environmental design, not confrontation.
