# Mealtime Positioning for Dysphagia: A Caregiver Checklist

Positioning during meals is not a one-size-fits-all task. A useful caregiver plan should be based on the person’s swallowing assessment, physiology, prescribed food and drink textures, and current care-team instructions.

## Questions to ask before the next meal

Use this checklist with the person’s clinician, dietitian, or swallowing-care team:

- Has a comprehensive swallowing assessment been completed, and which findings should guide positioning?
- What exact position should the person use? Does it need to change for different textures, fatigue, or other circumstances?
- Which cues should the caregiver give, and what assistance is required?
- Which food and drink textures have been prescribed?
- What observable changes mean the meal should stop?
- When and how should the caregiver escalate concerns to the care team?
- Is a referral for seating or swallowing support needed?

Write down the answers, identify who to contact if the plan changes, and keep the instructions with the meal records.

## Supporting the meal

- Set up the prescribed textures, position, equipment, cues, and assistance before serving food or drink.
- Follow only the positioning and assistance instructions provided for this person.
- Remain attentive during the meal and follow the plan for stopping and escalation.
- Do not use the absence of a cough as proof that swallowing is safe.
- Do not improvise hands-on maneuvers, equipment modifications, or a different posture without clinician guidance.

If the instructions do not explain how the person should be supported in their seat, ask for a referral rather than choosing a generic setup. A seating referral should consider the person’s actual seating and swallowing needs.

## What the primary sources support

ASHA’s [Adult Dysphagia portal](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/adult-dysphagia/) states that management follows a comprehensive assessment, individual needs vary, and the effects of postural techniques depend on an individual’s physiology. Chin-down positioning can reduce penetration or aspiration in some patients, but may be less effective when dysphagia is more severe. Silent aspiration can occur without overt signs. These findings support individualized clinician direction rather than a fixed positioning rule.

The randomized trial [reported by Robbins and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18378947/) studied 515 adults with dementia or Parkinson disease who aspirated thin liquid and followed them for 3 months. It found no statistically significant difference between chin-down and thickened-liquid groups. This does not establish equivalence between the approaches, universal prevention, or generalization to people with stroke. The trial DOI is [10.7326/0003-4819-148-7-200804010-00007](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-148-7-200804010-00007). The result should not be used alone to select a posture or liquid for an individual.

## After the meal

Record the position, textures, cues, and assistance used, along with any overt changes noticed during the meal. Share relevant observations with the care team rather than assuming they establish what happened during swallowing. If the plan is unclear, clarify it before the next meal.
